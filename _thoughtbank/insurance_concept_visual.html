The bet in one sentence. The property insurance workflow is being rebuilt around AI agents right now, and the first data platform that offers a MCP-native, parcel-level, calibrated risk substrate with a pre-loss anchor and a direct settlement rail will own the underwriting-to-claims stack before Verisk or CoreLogic retrofit their APIs to compete.

1. Three products, one substrate

Three distinct insurance products sit on the same atom substrate. They are not independent bets; they compound on each other. Underwriting creates the pre-loss anchor that enables claims handling. Claims handling produces the outcome data that calibrates underwriting. The parametric oracle is the automated version of the same loop.

2. The compounding flywheel

The three wedges are not three separate revenue lines. They form a single self-reinforcing loop. An insurer that enters at wedge 1 generates the pre-loss anchor automatically. Once a loss event occurs, the anchor enables AI-native adjudication. The settled outcome feeds calibration on the risk atoms. Calibration makes the underwriting signal sharper, which improves loss ratios, which justifies lower premiums or better terms, which attracts more insurers into the loop.

4. Wedge 2 — Parametric oracle and payment

A parametric insurance product pays automatically when a measured parameter crosses a threshold. The product depends entirely on having a trusted, independent oracle to verify whether the trigger fired. A Hauska atom is exactly this: sourced, calibrated, anchored, tamper-evident. The Circle rail handles the resulting payment. The combination — oracle plus settlement — is something no incumbent data provider offers.

The oracle moat

Once a parametric product is built on Hauska's trigger atoms, the product cannot function without us. The insurer's policy form references our oracle. Switching means rewriting the policy form, re-filing with the DoI, and migrating all in-force policies. The oracle is stickier than a data API contract by an order of magnitude.

Revenue model

  • Oracle subscription: per active parametric policy per month (e.g. $3–5/policy/month)
  • Settlement take rate: 0.5–1% of disbursement value, same logic as a payment network
  • Both can apply to the same policy; the oracle fee covers the monitoring cost, the take rate covers the payment infrastructure

Competitive context

Arbol, Etherisc, and Nayms are building parametric DeFi insurance using synthetic weather indices and low-granularity public data. The data quality is their biggest weakness — basis risk (the trigger fires but the actual loss did not, or vice versa) is high. Hauska's parcel-level, Cotality-sourced, calibrated atoms reduce basis risk structurally. That is the pitch to any insurer evaluating parametric products: lower basis risk means a product that actually pays when it should and doesn't pay when it shouldn't, which is the entire value proposition.

5. Wedge 3 — AI-native claims handling

The pre-loss anchor is the foundational insight

At policy binding, the underwriting atoms for the parcel are anchored — flood exposure, hazard score, permit history, property condition, encumbrances. Tamper-evident. Timestamped. Immutable. Neither the insured nor the insurer can later dispute what the property's state was at binding. This record did not exist before. Both sides in a disputed claim have been arguing from memory, photos, and competing expert opinions. The anchored atom is an independent third party with no side.

The claims data room (large commercial losses)

Large commercial and catastrophe losses involve multiple parties simultaneously: the insured, the primary carrier, an adjusting firm, and the reinsurer on the ceded portion. Today the evidentiary record is a chaos of email threads, PDFs, and competing expert reports. Dispute is the default state of a large commercial claim.

A Hauska claims data room is a permissioned atom space where every party sees exactly what their access policy allows, the adjuster appends findings atoms as the claim progresses, and the reinsurer sees only the atoms covering the ceded portion. The pre-loss anchor is the shared, undisputed foundation. Nobody re-litigates the baseline.

Subrogation as a side effect

After paying a claim, a carrier can subrogate — recover from the third party whose actions caused the loss. The Hauska place graph is already recording causal relationships between nodes. A flood event that the graph links to upstream development's impervious cover increase (a pending-event atom that became historical) is a subrogation evidence package produced as a side effect of normal operation. The carrier does not need to build the case from scratch; the atoms have already assembled it.

Economics

Average cost to handle a residential property claim: $300–800 in adjuster time, plus the report stack (CLUE, field inspection, contractor estimates). AI-native claims handling targets 60–75% cost reduction on straightforward claims (the majority), with human escalation for complex losses. Revenue model: per-claim fee ($50–150 for AI-handled claims, plus data room subscription for large commercial losses).

6. The pre-loss property certificate as a standalone product

The pre-loss anchor is a product independent of any specific claim. A tamper-evident, blockchain-anchored record of a property's condition at a specific date has value in at least four contexts beyond insurance.

Mortgage originationLenderCondition at origination; reduces repurchase risk on fraudulent applications

This is a Layer 2 product with a natural Layer 1 entry: a basic parcel snapshot at low confidence (public-record data only, free tier) vs a full anchored certificate with all Cotality layers and blockchain proof (paid tier). The insurance market buys the full version; the RE market is a natural second buyer since the same atom corpus serves both.
