# Why There Is Nothing Else Like This on Your Shelf

The data about every place in America exists and does not function. The vendors around that problem each hold one piece of it. Nobody sells the fix. Here is the shape of the gap, and the four claims no competitor can make.

## The terrain

**The data holders.** Over three thousand counties and municipalities hold the data: published as PDF, fragmented across every county line, siloed inside each government, stale with no freshness signal, asserted with no provenance. Forty-five years of national-standardization calls, no mandate, every federal attempt at a national layer dead.

**The aggregators.** National parcel coverage, but what they sell is flat rows: geometry plus attributes. No reasoning, no citation chain, no currency checking. Their coverage is achieved by scraping and digitizing whatever exists, so their pipeline inherits every failure above and papers over it.

**The workflow incumbents.** Permit systems, work-order systems, assessment hosting. Each is a system of transaction for one silo. None is a system of record about the place itself, and some contractually restrict a government from publishing its own data.

**The scale players.** Big-government platforms priced for big government, skipping the thousands of small and mid-size cities entirely.

## The evidence, all of it checkable

Thirty-five listings on this storefront as of July 2026. None in jurisdictional intelligence, property reasoning, or plan review. The nearest adjacency is a traffic dashboard.

Roughly 39,000 local governments; about 10,000 of 20,000 permitting jurisdictions do not publish permits online at all, and no US jurisdiction serves its zoning rules through a public API. A federal pilot measured AI accuracy on public data at 0 to 2 percent without a machine interface, and about 95 percent with one.

Six federal attempts at a national property-data layer since 1980, none implemented. The federal government now licenses commercial parcel data for its own use.

The one state that mandates measurement prices a public records request at roughly $265 and 3.5 staff hours, and parcel and permit records are among the two most-requested categories. Cities that publish the high-demand data saw request volume drop 30 to 43 percent in the one large study.
