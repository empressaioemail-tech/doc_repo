# R16 — Public records request burden: volumes, cost, and the property-related share

Research note, compiled 2026-08-07. Public-web sources only. Every figure is labeled by evidence grade (key at the end).

## Bottom line

The best-measured jurisdiction in the country, Washington State, shows its 236 highest-volume local and state agencies receiving 483,861 public records requests in 2024 and spending more than $127 million and 1.7 million staff hours to answer them, which works out to roughly $265 and 3.5 staff hours per request; cities and towns alone took 43% of that volume. No national dataset exists, but the largest independent analysis (Sunlight Foundation, 236,616 requests across 52 mid-sized cities) found that parcel records, permits, and plans are one of the two most-demanded record categories, second only to police records, and an older New Jersey clerk survey puts property-adjacent records (permits, building, tax, planning/zoning) at a combined majority of named request types. The same Sunlight research is the strongest publish-to-deflect evidence found: cities that adopted an open data policy saw roughly a 30% drop in monthly request volume, rising to 43% under a robust policy, with volume continuing to fall about 4 requests per month for each month the policy is in place. Vendor evidence points the same direction at smaller scale (Bay County, MI cut volume 23% by deflecting requests toward already-published records; GovQA markets 20-60% deflection). Texas has no statewide request census, but Austin's 2023 city audit documents about 30,000 requests a year citywide with a 120-plus-person distributed fulfillment apparatus, and the Texas AG issued over 40,000 open-records letter rulings in 2023 on disputed requests alone. The weakest evidence is on the informal counter-and-phone burden: it is widely described (permit-tech job descriptions say fielding routine property questions "can account for most of the daily workload") but nowhere quantified in hours; that absence is itself a finding.

## 1. Request volumes at local governments

**Washington State (the only mandatory, statewide, annually published census).** Since 2017, any Washington agency spending over $100,000/year on records requests must report 15 metrics to the legislature's Joint Legislative Audit and Review Committee (JLARC). For calendar 2024: 236 reporting agencies received 483,861 requests; cities and towns received the highest share, 208,949 requests (43% of the statewide total). 57% of requests closed within five days; average closure 24 days; 75% fulfilled electronically. [government-document] Caveat stated in the report itself: JLARC compiles but does not independently verify agency submissions, and the $100k threshold means this is the high-volume subset, not all local governments. (JLARC 2024 Public Records Reporting, published 2025: leg.wa.gov/jlarc/reports/2025/PubRecordsData/default.html)

**Austin, TX.** The Office of the City Auditor's August 2023 audit found APD received more than 82,000 public information requests from 2018-2022 (about 20,000 in a typical year, more than double the rest of the city) versus just over 39,000 for all other city operations combined (about 10,000/year), i.e. roughly 30,000 requests/year citywide. APD carried a backlog of about 20,000 unfulfilled requests. [government-document, as reported by press] (Austin Monitor 2023-08, austinmonitor.com/stories/2023/08/audit-finds-apd-backlog-of-20000-public-information-requests/; Austin Chronicle 2023-09-06; audit PDF: services.austintexas.gov/edims/document.cfm?id=414051)

**San Diego Development Services Department.** The city's own page states DSD "receives hundreds of PRA requests each month" — and because DSD is the permitting/plans/code-enforcement department, essentially all of that volume is property-related. [government-document, order-of-magnitude only] (sandiego.gov/development-services/pra-requests, current 2026)

**Bay County, MI** (pop. ~103,000): 144 requests/month baseline (~1,700/year) per a JustFOIA case study. [vendor-claim] (justfoia.com/case-study/bay-county-cuts-request-volume-with-deflection/, ~2024-2025)

**Trend data.** GovQA/Granicus "Peers in Public Records" survey and PiPRIndex (vendor, ~500 anonymized agencies, ~240 after outlier removal): most respondents estimate volume up 25-50% over three years; complexity index up 173% from Q1 2018 to Q4 2021. [vendor-claim] (granicus.com/blog/govqa-piprindex/, 2021-2022). The eScribe/IIMC 2026 State of the Clerk's Office Report (702 clerk respondents): three in four municipalities report request growth over the past two years; 84-86% of municipalities over 10,000 population report growth; 61% of those under 10,000. [vendor-claim, survey with association partner] (escribemeetings.com/blog/clerk-records-request-challenges/, 2026)

**Property-related share.** The Sunlight Foundation analysis of 110,063 requests from 33 cities (machine-classified into 60 categories, drawn from a 236,616-request / 52-city corpus sourced largely from NextRequest portals) found the two most-demanded categories were police incident reports and "parcel records, permits, or plans." [measured-study; published 2018-10, still the only category census of its kind found] (sunlightfoundation.com/2018/10/16/results-from-analyzing-public-record-requests/). The Monmouth University poll of 260 NJ municipal clerks found the most-cited request types were permits (24%), council minutes/agendas (23%), building/construction (19%), ordinances (17%), tax records (15%), planning/zoning (14%), police (12%) — property-adjacent categories dominating — with attorneys the second-largest requester group after residents. [measured-study; fielded 2009-2010, included because nothing newer breaks out categories; directional only] (monmouth.edu/polling-institute/reports/MonmouthPoll_NJOPRA_013111/). **Honest absence: no 2019-2026 source publishes the property-related percentage of local records requests. The Washington JLARC metrics do not categorize by record type.** Florida's municipal lien search industry (title companies ordering per-sale searches of unrecorded liens, open permits, code violations from every city) is indirect evidence that property-transaction demand is a standing per-sale workload for city staff, but no aggregate volume data was found. [anecdotal] (e.g. delraybeachfl.gov municipal lien searches page)

## 2. Fulfillment cost in staff time and dollars

**Washington JLARC, 2024 data:** $127+ million and 1.7 million staff hours across 236 agencies and 483,861 requests. Derived averages: ~$263-265 and ~3.5 staff hours per request (division of reported totals; not a JLARC-published per-request statistic). State agencies had the highest costs ($45M, 35% of total); cities carried the largest request count. [government-document; per-request figures derived] Prior years: $101M (2019), $100M (2021), so the burden has grown ~27% in five years on this reporting base. [government-document]

**Granicus analysis of the 2018 JLARC data** (vendor blog over government data): 350,000+ requests, 1M+ staff hours across 204 agencies, average 2.83 hours per request, an estimated 480-595 statewide FTEs devoted to records fulfillment, $4.5M+ in litigation costs; school districts averaged ~23 hours per request, the highest of any agency type. It also notes the structural problem: Washington cost recovery is limited to copying/delivery, so staff time, the dominant cost, is unrecoverable. [vendor-claim analyzing government-document data] (granicus.com/blog/jlarc-metrics-explained-...)

**Austin staffing:** the 2023 audit press coverage reports ~120 staff across departments handle the non-APD ~10,000 requests/year, versus 6 staff for APD's ~20,000. Documented headcount, not a dollar figure; at loaded municipal labor rates it implies a multi-million-dollar annual function for one city. [government-document via press; dollar inference derived]

**Honest absence:** no NAGARA, ICMA, or municipal-league study from 2019-2026 publishes a national per-request dollar cost for local governments. Washington's JLARC series is the only recurring measured baseline; everything else is vendor benchmarking behind lead-gen forms (e.g. Granicus 2022 Public Records Benchmark Report).

## 3. The adjacent burden: counter and phone traffic that never becomes a request

This is the thinnest evidence area, and the absence is the finding: **no survey or study from 2019-2026 quantifies staff hours spent answering routine property questions at the counter or phone.** What exists is qualitative:

- Permit technician role documentation: fielding questions about permit status, submittal requirements, and which permits a project needs "can account for most of the daily workload" in busy departments. [anecdotal/occupational description] (actasolutions.io/blog/what-is-a-permit-technician, ~2025; City of Saratoga permit technician class spec, updated April 2025)
- eScribe/IIMC 2026 survey: 44% of clerks name locating records as the top fulfillment delay; clerks describe balancing "public counter time" against fulfillment; 75% already proactively publish some records online. No hour counts. [vendor-claim with association partner]
- Monmouth 2010 NJ survey: 61% of clerk offices spent under two hours/week on formal OPRA work, 21% over five hours, with single-person offices hit disproportionately, implying the formal-request number understates total public-facing records work in small towns. [measured-study, pre-window]
- Cities institutionalize the informal channel by monetizing slices of it: zoning verification letters and municipal lien searches are fee-for-service products precisely because the question volume is recurring (Atlanta routes ZVLs through Accela; Florida cities run standing lien-search queues at 5-7 business days turnaround). [government-document, volume unquantified]

## 4. Publish-to-deflect evidence

**The strongest evidence found, and it is directly on point.** Sunlight Foundation (published 2018-10-09; regression over 236,616 requests, 52 mid-sized cities, 14 states, data back to 2009): adopting an open data policy is associated with **approximately a 30% decrease in monthly public records requests**; a robust open data portal hosting the high-demand datasets showed a 33% reduction versus controls; a robust open data policy showed a 43% reduction in monthly requests per 10,000 residents; and each additional month a policy is in place is associated with ~4 fewer requests than the month before. The companion analysis explicitly recommends cities proactively release the two top-demand categories, police incident reports and **parcel/permit records**, to capture the savings. [measured-study; 2018 publication, no successor study found] (sunlightfoundation.com/2018/10/09/research-cities-save-time-on-records-requests-by-doing-open-data-right/)

**Bay County, MI (JustFOIA case study):** request volume down 23% over two years by deflecting misrouted requests and pointing requesters at already-published records; budget-document requests went from 10-15/month to "virtually none" once published. No cost figures. [vendor-claim]

**GovQA/Granicus marketing:** claims automatic deflection/merging "can reduce volume by 20-60%." [vendor-claim, unaudited]

**Government self-testimony without before/after numbers:** San Diego DSD's PRA page steers requesters to OpenDSD/Permit Finder/open data for anything post-2003, i.e. the city treats published permit data as its request-deflection front line [government-document]; Washington's PRA (RCW 42.56.520) lets an agency satisfy a request by providing a link to the record posted online, making publication a legally recognized fulfillment channel [government-document] (mrsc.org). **No county recorder/assessor before-and-after study of online publication was found; that specific gap is open.**

## 5. Texas specifically

- **No statewide request census exists.** Texas has no JLARC equivalent; nobody counts total PIA requests received by Texas cities and counties. [honest absence]
- **Austin** is the best-documented Texas data point: ~30,000 requests/year citywide (APD ~20,000 with 6 staff; rest of city ~10,000 with ~120 staff involved), 20,000-request APD backlog, per the August 2023 city audit, which recommended centralizing PIR management. [government-document]
- **Texas AG letter rulings:** more than 40,000 open-records letter rulings issued in 2023. A ruling is generated only when a governmental body asks to withhold information, so this is a floor on the volume of contested requests statewide and implies total request volume orders of magnitude higher. HB 3033 (88th Legislature) forced these rulings into a searchable public database from December 2023. [government-document, figure as reported on AG pages; direct page fetch was blocked, so treat the precise count as unverified-at-source]
- **TML** publishes PIA guides and has lobbied on the compliance burden, but **no TML survey quantifying request volumes or fulfillment cost at member cities was found**. [honest absence]
- Texas cost-recovery rules (AG PIA Handbook 2024) allow charging for copies and, above thresholds, labor, but like Washington the routine-request labor burden is largely unrecoverable in practice. [government-document]

## Evidence-grade key

- **measured-study**: independent analysis with stated methodology (Sunlight Foundation 2018; Monmouth University 2010).
- **government-document**: official report, audit, statute, or agency webpage (WA JLARC 2019-2024; Austin City Auditor 2023; Texas AG; San Diego DSD; MRSC).
- **vendor-claim**: figures published by records-request software vendors or their partnered surveys (Granicus/GovQA, JustFOIA, eScribe/IIMC). Directionally useful, commercially motivated, unaudited.
- **anecdotal**: occupational descriptions, individual city practices, industry existence proofs.
- Derived figures (JLARC per-request averages) are divisions of published totals, labeled where used.

## The county's own incentive to publish

The evidence assembles into a clean economic argument even though no single source states it. On the cost side, the only mandatory measurement regime in the country prices records fulfillment at roughly $265 and 3.5 staff hours per request for high-volume agencies, growing about 27% over five years, with cities carrying the largest request share and nearly all of that labor cost legally unrecoverable from requesters. On the demand side, the only large category census puts parcel records, permits, and plans in the top two request categories, and the everyday texture of the work (permit counters, zoning verification letters, Florida's entire municipal lien-search queue) shows property questions arriving as a standing, transaction-driven load that scales with real estate activity, not with anything the government controls. A county that does nothing is signing up for a per-request cost structure on a demand curve it cannot cap.

The deflection evidence says publication is the one lever with a measured payoff. The Sunlight regressions found a 30-43% reduction in monthly request volume for cities that publish the high-demand data well, compounding month over month, and specifically named parcel and permit records as the category to release proactively. The vendor ecosystem independently converges on the same numbers from the operational side (Bay County's 23%, GovQA's 20-60% deflection marketing), and Washington law already treats a posted record with a link as a completed response. So the pitch to a county is not transparency altruism, it is unit economics: every property record class moved from request-fulfillment to self-service converts a ~$265, 3.5-hour, 24-day-average-closure transaction into a zero-marginal-cost page view, and the measured deflection rates suggest a third or more of the property-related queue is convertible.

Two gaps matter for selling this honestly. First, nobody has published a recorder- or assessor-specific before-and-after study, and the property-related percentage of local request volume has not been measured anywhere since 2018, so a pilot county that instruments its own request log before and after publication would own the reference number for the whole market. Second, the largest burden may be the one that never enters any log: the counter and phone traffic that permit-tech job descriptions call the bulk of the daily workload is quantified nowhere, which means the formal-request savings estimate is a floor, and the county's own front-line staff are the only people who can testify to the ceiling. Both gaps are arguments for structuring any records-publication engagement to measure its own deflection effect from day one.
