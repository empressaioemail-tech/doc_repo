# R20 — What killed every serious attempt at a US national parcel database

## Bottom line

Every serious attempt at a national land parcel database since 1980 has died in the same narrow corridor: the federal government has a genuine need, repeatedly documented, but no agency has ever held the combination of statutory authority, sustained appropriation, and leverage over the roughly 3,000 counties that actually make parcels. The 1980 NRC multipurpose cadastre report produced follow-up studies and local experiments but no federal agency or money. The 2007 NRC report declared a national system "necessary, feasible, and affordable" at about $300M plus under $100M per year, laid out nine concrete recommendations including named federal and national parcel coordinators, and not one recommendation was implemented; CRS attributed the stall to funding, federalism, privacy statutes, and local governments' rational refusal to give away self-funded data. The only parcel legislation that ever passed, the FLAIR Act, took 17 years, rode a 2022 omnibus, and covers federal lands only. The federal operational need was ultimately met by purchase, not construction: DHS's HIFLD Secure licenses LightBox's commercial nationwide parcel fabric behind a data use agreement, which is an admission that the public national layer failed. The Geospatial Data Act of 2018 codified coordination machinery (FGDC, NGDA themes, annual reporting) but contains no parcel program; the NGDA Cadastre theme under BLM covers federal cadastral data such as PLSS, not local parcels. Meanwhile roughly 31 states quietly built the thing bottom-up, and every state that succeeded fast did it by either doing the mapping itself (Montana, Tennessee) or pairing a statutory mandate with recurring money flowing to counties (Wisconsin, Vermont, Florida). The consistent lesson: parcels get built where the entity that needs the data either makes it or pays the maker on a recurring basis; coordination without authority or money has failed for 45 years straight.

## Attempt 1: NRC, "Need for a Multipurpose Cadastre" (1980)

What it proposed. A nationwide multipurpose cadastre: parcel-indexed land records supporting titling, assessment/taxation, and land use management, built on geodetic control and base maps. It recommended creating a federal surveying and mapping agency to coordinate geodetic, cadastral, and mapping activities, plus an interagency working group spanning federal, state, local, and private actors. A companion NRC study, "Procedures and Standards for a Multipurpose Cadastre" (1983), specified implementation procedures.

Who was supposed to pay. Primarily local government (as record keepers) with federal coordination and standards; the reports assumed federal institutional leadership that never materialized.

What actually happened. No federal surveying and mapping agency was created and no appropriation followed. The concept devolved into local land information system experiments through the 1980s, with Dane County, Wisconsin as the flagship testbed, and later fed FGDC cadastral data standards. The 2007 NRC report's background chapter states flatly that a fully operational multipurpose cadastre was never realized, citing capacity concerns from 1980 that persisted alongside "little to no resource commitment at the federal level."

Failure cause. Coordination without authority plus no sustained appropriation. The report asked for a new federal institution in an era hostile to creating one, and offered local governments standards but no money.

## Attempt 2: NRC, "National Land Parcel Data: A Vision for the Future" (2007)

What it proposed. Not a central database but a federated, distributed system: data held by stewards (counties, states, federal agencies), aggregated virtually through web services, nationally accessible. Nine recommendations, the load-bearing ones being: (1) appoint both a Federal Land Parcel Coordinator (for the ~650M acres of federal land) and a National Land Parcel Coordinator (all sources), with a panel to decide whether BLM had the necessary authority and capacity; (3) a single authoritative federal-lands parcel database (Interior then ran 26 financial systems and over 100 property systems); (4) a national business plan on the FGDC Cadastral Subcommittee template; (6) amend Census Title 13 to release building address coordinates; (7) a state parcel coordinator in every state via the Fifty States Initiative ("federation by mandate"); (8) an intergovernmental funding program; (9) tie eligibility for property-related federal funds (disaster relief, CDBG) to submission of digital parcel data.

Who was supposed to pay. Blended: state/local assessment budgets already carrying most of the cost (~70 percent of parcels already digitized per the committee), federal seed money of roughly $240M to finish conversion, ~$300M total to initial completion, under $100M per year recurring (Stage and von Meyer 2007 estimates). The Western Governors' Association separately estimated $350M over three years (cited in CRS R40717).

What actually happened. Nothing structural. No coordinator was appointed, the BLM authority panel was never convened, no funding was authorized, Title 13 was not amended, and the grant-conditioning recommendation (the one with real teeth) was never attempted. CRS confirmed in 2011 that "no panel was established to implement these recommendations." The report's own diagnosis proved prophetic: the question was never need, resources, or authority, "but rather whether it has the motivation and incentives to confront difficult institutional and financial obstacles," and the NRC concluded the lack of political will might be the hardest hurdle.

Failure cause. No statute, no appropriation, no champion. The one forcing mechanism proposed (conditioning federal grants) was politically untouchable because it read as an unfunded mandate on counties.

## Attempt 3: CRS R40717 and the FLAIR Act legislative thread (2009 to 2022)

What it analyzed. CRS Report R40717, "Issues Regarding a National Land Parcel Database" (updated May 13, 2011), is the closest thing to an official post-mortem. Issues identified: funding (new money required; WGA's $350M figure); federalism and local incentives (counties feared loss of accuracy control, objected to public-domain release of data they self-funded and in some cases sell, and distrusted participation without tangible benefit); privacy statutes (Census Title 13 confidentiality; 2008 Farm Bill Section 1619 restrictions); and coordination failure (BLM was nominal FGDC cadastral steward yet the 2007 NRC found no coordinated approach even to federally managed parcel data). Dodd-Frank (P.L. 111-203, 2010) added a parcel-number requirement to HMDA data, a small foreclosure-crisis-driven fragment of the vision.

Legislative outcome. The Federal Land Asset Inventory Reform (FLAIR) Act, first written in 2005 and reintroduced in at least seven Congresses without passing standalone, finally became law as a provision of the Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2023 (H.R. 2617, signed December 29, 2022). It directs Interior to build a multipurpose cadastre of federal real property only. NSGIC noted in 2025 that implementation of the related MAPLands Act mapping work has been "slower than stipulated."

Failure cause. Scope collapse plus no appropriation. The only politically passable slice was the federal-lands-only inventory, because it implicates no county, no privacy statute, and no intergovernmental money; even that took 17 years and an omnibus vehicle.

## Attempt 4: HUD's National Parcel Database feasibility project (2010 to 2013)

What it proposed. In 2010, motivated by the foreclosure crisis, HUD contracted Abt Associates with Fairview Industries and Smart Data Strategies to test assembling standardized county assessor data for 127 targeted counties, standardize it into a common schema, and assess scaling to a national database. Final report: "The Feasibility of Developing a National Parcel Database: County Data Records Project Final Report" (published 2013; data vintage 2010-2011; ~60 GB across 127 jurisdictions).

What actually happened. The pilot demonstrated the mechanics (county-direct acquisition and standardization are laborious but doable) and then stopped. No follow-on appropriation, no operational HUD parcel program; the dataset sits as an archived research artifact. The county-by-county heterogeneity of formats, access terms, and licensing that the project documented is precisely the cost structure that commercial aggregators (Boundary Solutions, CoreLogic, Digital Map Products, later LightBox and Regrid) monetized instead.

Failure cause. Pilot without a program: one-time research money, no statutory home, and the motivating crisis receded before institutionalization. It proved feasibility and thereby handed the blueprint to the private sector.

## Attempt 5: DHS, HIFLD, and the 2016 National Parcel Data Summit

What happened. DHS and the FGDC HIFLD Subcommittee convened a National Parcel Data Summit in Reston, Virginia in 2016. Its recommendations rhymed with 2007: a National Parcel Coordination Office overseeing a national parcel business plan, with competitive grant programs compensating states for coordinating and standardizing their counties' data. Briefed to the FGDC Coordination Group (July 2016) and NGAC (September 2016).

What actually happened. No coordination office, no grant program. The federal operational need was instead satisfied commercially: HIFLD Secure includes commercially licensed nationwide parcel data from LightBox (National ParcelMap Data Portal fulfillment framework agreement, December 2019), accessible only with an account and approved data use agreement. A national parcel layer for federal use exists today, but it is licensed, access-restricted, and non-public. Even the free HIFLD Open side entered an uncertain transition in 2025 per industry reporting. On the "Fair Market Value" thread: no documented federal FMV national parcel initiative surfaced in public sources; treat any such claim as unverified.

Failure cause. Substitution. Once DHS could license a commercial fabric, the marginal federal urgency for a public national layer collapsed. Summit recommendations required money and an office nobody was authorized to create; buying a license required only procurement.

## Attempt 6 (current posture): the Geospatial Data Act era (2018 to present)

The Geospatial Data Act of 2018 (P.L. 115-254; 43 U.S.C. 2801 et seq.) codified the FGDC, the NSDI, NGDA data themes with lead agencies, GeoPlatform, and mandatory annual reporting with OIG audits. It does not mention or fund a national parcel database. Parcels live inside the NGDA Cadastre theme, whose lead is DOI/BLM, and whose federal content is essentially the Public Land Survey System fabric and federal land records, not local tax parcels. The current federal posture: coordination machinery exists and is statutory; parcel content is out of federal scope except on federal land (FLAIR/MAPLands, running slow); federal operational consumption of nationwide private parcels happens through the LightBox license inside HIFLD Secure; and the advocacy locus has shifted to NSGIC, whose 2025 paper "The Case for a National Parcels Dataset" asks Congress only for modest supports. NSGIC's 2023 Geospatial Maturity Assessment reported 31 states with statewide parcel coverage, up from 29 in 2021 — the national layer is being assembled as a federation of state programs plus commercial aggregators, exactly the architecture the 2007 NRC described, minus the federal coordinator and federal money.

## State-level contrast: where statewide parcels actually got built

Roughly 31 states now report statewide parcel coverage (NSGIC GMA 2023), and NSGIC's 2025 paper says most states have parcel data for at least 80 percent of jurisdictions. The instructive split is who does the work and whether recurring money reaches the mapmaker.

**State-as-mapmaker (strong form).** Montana: the Department of Revenue itself maintains tax parcels statewide (it runs mass appraisal for the state) with the State Library integrating and publishing the Montana Cadastral Framework; only five counties self-maintain. Tennessee: statewide program since a 1996 Comptroller pilot; the Tennessee Base Mapping Program created the base layers, and the Comptroller's Division of Property Assessments still performs parcel maintenance directly for counties that never stood up GIS, which is why coverage is 95 of 95 counties. (No authoritative all-in program cost surfaced for either; treat any dollar figure as unverified.) Florida is a funded-mandate variant: the Department of Revenue collects parcel GIS from all 67 county property appraisers every April as part of tax-roll submission standards and publishes a statewide layer annually; compliance rides the property-tax oversight relationship.

**Mandate plus recurring grants.** Wisconsin: Act 20 of 2013 directed the Department of Administration to build a statewide digital parcel map and boosted Wisconsin Land Information Program grants to counties (funded by register-of-deeds recording fees, so recurring and off-appropriation; strategic-initiative grants conditioned on hitting "Searchable Format" standardization benchmarks). Eleven annual versions later, V10 (June 2024) published 3.56M standardized parcels statewide. Vermont: Act 158 of 2015 created a Statewide Property Parcel Mapping Program in statute (19 V.S.A. § 44), funded through VTrans, which contracted 10 private mapping vendors to rebuild or standardize parcels for all ~255 municipalities over three years (complete December 2019), then converted to a maintenance program at VCGI. Massachusetts: MassGIS ran a FY2011-2013 standardization project across all municipalities (funding tied to Next Generation E-911), completing all but Boston by July 2013.

**Coordinated-voluntary.** North Carolina: the NC Parcels project began in 2009 under the state GIS coordinating council, seeded by an EPA grant, aggregating all 100 counties into a standardized public-domain layer; it worked through relationships and a strong open-records culture but has visibly struggled for sustainable funding. Simultaneously the only state to earn an "A" in NSGIC's 2023 GMA and a cautionary tale about voluntary programs' funding fragility. Utah: UGRC publishes statewide parcels via a long-standing cooperative arrangement. Texas: the weakest of this cohort by design; StratMap added a Land Parcels program after the 2016 GIO report identified statewide parcels as the single greatest state-agency data need, and TxGIO now translates 245+ appraisal-district datasets into a common schema annually via vendor contract, but participation is voluntary, coverage is incomplete, and there is no county mandate (exactly why TxGIO/CAD-direct acquisition remains the working posture for authoritative Texas parcels).

**What the funded-mandate states did differently.** Three moves recur. First, they attached parcels to an existing money-and-compliance pipe (property tax oversight in Florida and Montana, recording fees in Wisconsin, E-911 in Massachusetts, the transportation budget in Vermont) rather than a standalone GIS appropriation that could be zeroed out. Second, they paid the actual mapmaker: grants conditioned on standards, or the state doing the mapping for low-capacity jurisdictions, converting the weakest counties from veto points into service recipients. Third, they legislated the schema, so aggregation is a compliance artifact rather than an annual negotiation. Voluntary states got to 80-100 percent only where a coordinating council plus open-records law substituted for mandate (North Carolina), and stalled below that elsewhere (Texas).

## Premortem lessons for a bottom-up national layer

The forty-five-year federal record is a controlled experiment in what does not work: advisory reports without appropriations (1980, 2007), coordination offices proposed without statutory authority (2007, 2016), pilots without programs (HUD 2010-2013), and legislation that survives only after being scoped down to land the federal government already owns (FLAIR, 2022). A private, product-funded, jurisdiction-by-jurisdiction approach structurally sidesteps the three deadliest causes. It needs no appropriation, so it cannot die in an authorization-without-funding gap. It asserts no mandate over counties, so it never triggers the federalism and unfunded-mandate immune response that killed grant conditioning in 2007. And it has a paying use case per jurisdiction rather than a diffuse national benefit, which is exactly the incentive structure the NRC identified as missing: every federal attempt failed because the entity that needed the data nationally was not the entity that made it locally, and no one closed that loop with money. A product that pays its own onboarding cost per jurisdiction closes the loop transaction by transaction, which is also how LightBox and Regrid actually built the only working national fabrics.

Two failure causes still apply with full force. The first is maintenance economics, the quiet killer behind every "completed" effort: the 2007 NRC priced recurring maintenance at up to a third of initial build cost every single year, Wisconsin needs a permanent recording-fee stream and eleven annual versions to stay current, and North Carolina, the best voluntary program in the country, still scrambles for cost-share to keep its aggregate alive. A jurisdiction onboarded once and not refreshed decays toward the same staleness that made pre-program state layers useless; the per-jurisdiction unit economics have to price the refresh loop, not the first pass, and the sub-$200-per-jurisdiction discipline matters most on the Nth refresh, not the first crawl. The second persistent cause is source-access friction as a business model on the other side: counties and their vendors (the eCode360/General Code posture, CAD data-sales operations) have a documented, rational history of restricting exactly this data because they monetize it, which is the same local-incentive wall CRS described in 2011. Public-record acquisition postures blunt but do not eliminate this; expect the friction to be permanent and jurisdiction-specific rather than a solvable one-time obstacle.

The state contrast carries the sharpest design lesson: successful programs did not aggregate whatever counties happened to publish, they legislated or contracted a schema and paid or performed the standardization themselves, converting weak jurisdictions from veto points into service recipients. The bottom-up equivalent is owning the normalization layer and treating low-capacity jurisdictions as places where you do the work rather than wait for data, which is Tennessee's model translated to private economics. Conversely, the Texas experience warns against mistaking voluntary coverage percentages for a durable asset; coverage that depends on each source's continued goodwill is a rented layer. The moat position that survives this history is not possession of boundaries, which commercial fabrics already sell and states increasingly give away, but the compounding, per-jurisdiction reasoning and calibration built on top, because that is the one layer no failed federal attempt, no state program, and no commercial aggregator has ever produced.

Finally, watch the substitution dynamic that ended the federal story: the moment a good-enough licensed fabric existed, institutional demand for building the real thing evaporated. That cuts both ways for a bottom-up builder. It means the addressable buyer is anyone dissatisfied with flat commercial fabrics (which carry boundaries and assessor attributes but no jurisdictional reasoning), and it means a bottom-up layer must stay differentiated on what the fabrics cannot do, or it becomes the next thing substituted away. The federal government is not a competitor here and never will be; on the 45-year evidence, the realistic endgame for the public sector is 31-plus state federations of varying quality with no national coordinator, which leaves the integrated, reasoning-bearing national layer permanently open to whoever funds it one jurisdiction at a time.
