# R11 — API access to US property / permit / zoning data: what can actually be counted

## Bottom line

Nobody has produced an authoritative census of which US jurisdictions offer API access to property, permit, or zoning data — the organizations that tried (Sunlight Foundation, US City Open Data Census) are defunct, and no academic successor has counted at national scale. The best available proxies say API access is a thin crust on a large rock: roughly 5,000+ government ArcGIS servers exist across all levels of US government (federal to city) against roughly 39,000 general-purpose local governments, and the parcel-specific verified-endpoint lists number only in the low hundreds. Permit data is the starkest case: of ~20,000 permitting jurisdictions, a leading aggregator reaches ~2,750 by scraping (not APIs) and estimates ~10,000 jurisdictions do not publish permits online at all; the dominant permitting vendors (Accela, Tyler EnerGov) expose APIs only behind per-agency authorization, with no public anonymous tier. No US jurisdiction was found serving zoning geometry plus rules through its own public documented API; that layer exists only as commercial products (Zoneomics) and a manual academic digitization effort (National Zoning Atlas, ~9,000 of 33,295 jurisdictions, no public API). The one open standard for permit data, BLDS, is dead — its domain now redirects to an unrelated site and its repo carries an explicit "not actively maintained" notice. As of mid-2026, MCP servers for property data exist only at the federal level (Census, GPO) and from commercial aggregators (ATTOM, Jan 2026); no US city or county ships one. The honest headline: agent-consumable jurisdictional data is supplied almost entirely by private aggregators who scrape, not by jurisdictions who serve.

## 1. Censuses and surveys of local-government open data

**Finding: the counting institutions are dead; no 2021+ national census of local open-data APIs exists.**

- The Sunlight Foundation ceased operations September 2020; its site is a static archive. Its US City Open Data Census (with Open Knowledge International and Code for America, launched 2014) crowdsource-tracked dataset availability for on the order of 100 cities and was last substantively updated in the 2017-2018 cycle. [M, historical; defunct] (sunlightfoundation.com 2018-02-01; us-cities.survey.okfn.org, static)
- Post-2020 academic work is narrow: a 2024 arXiv study of high-value dataset identification examined nine large US city portals (NYC, LA, Chicago, Dallas, Austin, SF, Seattle et al.), all on Socrata, totaling ~17,000 datasets as of May 2024. A study of the API-rich elite, not a census. [M] (arxiv.org/pdf/2406.10541, 2024-06)
- Denominator context: the Census of Governments counts roughly 39,000 general-purpose local governments. [G] (census.gov/programs-surveys/cog, 2022 cycle)

**Honest answer: nobody has counted since ~2018.** The proxies below are the real evidence base.

## 2. The ArcGIS REST reality

**Finding: ArcGIS REST endpoints are the de facto "jurisdiction API" of the US, numbering in the low thousands of servers — and they are fragile enough that the main directory of them re-tests every link weekly.**

- The most complete enumeration anywhere is one retiree's curated PDF: Joseph Elfelt (mappingsupport.com) maintains a list of **5,000+ US government ArcGIS server addresses across federal, state, county, and city levels**, built over ~7 years. His own code re-scans every address weekly (three runs, hours apart); "bad links are fixed or flagged" in a new PDF every Wednesday. A directory that requires weekly automated re-verification is itself the measurement of endpoint churn. Grew from ~1,400 (2018-19) to ~3,500 (2022) to 5,000+ (2025). [M, informal method] (geohipster.com 2025-07-29; mappingsupport.com list PDF)
- Parcel-specific: UrbanKit Studio's Parcel Atlas lists **155 verified county parcel FeatureServer/MapServer endpoints across 50 states** — verified, but a tiny fraction of ~3,143 counties. States plainly that "not all counties publish ArcGIS REST services publicly. Some require authentication or only offer data through web map viewers"; roughly half of county servers publish MapServer only (no FeatureServer). [M, small-n; O] (urbankitstudio.com/tools/parcel-lookup, accessed 2026-08-07)
- Fragility documented but not systematically studied: Esri's June 2022 ArcGIS Online update changed hosted-service REST endpoint access [A]; community threads document REST URLs changing and layers vanishing [A]; OpenAddresses, which harvests from exactly these county endpoints, reports sources breaking for years at a time and "servers are unreliable and the data is constantly changing" as a structural condition. [A, at scale]
- Esri's own guidance leans **against** public endpoints: "It is always recommended to set the sharing property to private, group, or organization"; public items make the owner "responsible for all usage charges," and security best-practice docs advise shutting off anonymous portal access. Platform defaults and incentives push toward viewer-only. [V, vendor documentation]
- That private aggregation is the workaround is quantified by Regrid: **160M parcels, 3,229 counties**, assembled by aggregation and standardization of county sources — county-by-county acquisition exists precisely because uniform API access does not. [V] (regrid.com/nationwide-parcels)

**No one has enumerated public MapServer/FeatureServer endpoints with an academic method.** Elfelt's 5,000+ (all levels, all topics) and UrbanKit's 155 (county parcels, verified) bracket the honest range.

## 3. Permit data APIs

**Finding: vendor APIs exist but are per-agency-gated; public anonymous permit APIs are rare enough that the market leader in permit aggregation runs "self-healing scrapers" and a manual outreach program instead.**

- **Accela**: real public developer portal (developer.accela.com) with a REST API — but access is via OAuth2 app authorization against a specific agency's instance; each agency decides whether third-party apps can reach its data. No anonymous public tier. [V] Accela's open-data portal CivicData.com claimed "over 60 state and local governments" at its mid-2010s peak [V, stale]; the site today returns HTTP 403 to non-browser clients with no sign of active promotion. [O]
- **Tyler EnerGov** (600+ US/Canadian agencies [V]): no public developer portal; API documentation behind customer login; credentials require an active Tyler license plus professional services; no public sandbox. Third-party API report card grades Tyler "F" on developer accessibility. [A/V] The clinching anecdote: someone had to file a **FOIA request via MuckRock to obtain EnerGov API documentation** from LA County. [A] (muckrock.com/foi/.../energov-api-documentation-los-angeles-county-counsel-168653/)
- **OpenGov/ViewPoint, MyGov, CitizenServe**: no public developer portals or documented public data APIs surfaced. [A, absence, weakly held]
- **How many deployments enable public access: nobody has counted.** Best proxy is Shovels.ai (permit aggregator, $5M seed June 2025): coverage of **2,750+ jurisdictions (~85% of population) achieved via AI web scraping** (self-healing scrapers — an engineering response to endpoint fragility), against **~20,000 permitting jurisdictions nationally, of which ~10,000 don't publish permits online at all** (their "Project Storm" does manual outreach to offline jurisdictions). If public permit APIs were common, this architecture would not exist. [V, structurally revealing] (shovels.ai/blog/project-storm-...; shovels.ai/coverage; Commercial Observer 2025-06)
- A minority of large cities expose permits through Socrata/SODA open-data APIs (NYC, Chicago, Austin, SF et al.). Order of magnitude: dozens of cities, not thousands. [M, small-n]

## 4. Zoning APIs

**Finding: no US jurisdiction was found serving zoning geometry + rules through its own public documented API. Geometry-only layers exist on some city/county GIS servers; the rules layer exists only via a commercial vendor and a manual academic atlas.**

- **National Zoning Atlas** (Cornell-originated, Sara Bronin): has identified **33,295 zoning jurisdictions**, logged 102,000+ districts, read 1.11M+ pages of code, with **~9,000 jurisdictions live** on its interactive map. Method is fundamentally manual — human analysts read zoning texts and extract ~200 regulatory characteristics per district — itself the strongest evidence that the rules are not machine-readable at the source. **Access is viewer-first: no public API**; some state datasets (MT, NH, VA, TN, HI, MA) downloadable as GeoJSON/CSV via the Mercatus Center and an S3 index. [M] (zoningatlas.org/how; mercatus.org state atlas datasets; accessed 2026-08-07)
- **Zoneomics** is the only documented public zoning API found: commercial, paid, claiming coverage of **20,000-25,000 US municipalities**, serving zone code/district/type, permitted uses, dimensional standards, and (since late 2025) parcel boundaries and machine-readable conditional-control logic. Coverage and depth claims unverified. [V] (zoneomics.com/product/api; December 2025 release notes)
- Individual jurisdictions: many cities publish a zoning **geometry** layer (a district polygon with a code string), but the **rules** stay in PDF code text — the NZA's million pages read is the measurement of that gap. One state-level exception pattern: Massachusetts republishes NZA data through its state GIS portal. [A]

## 5. The standards graveyard

**Finding: BLDS is dead; Open311 partially survived; no zoning/parcel schema standard has achieved meaningful US local adoption.**

- **BLDS (Building & Land Development Specification)**: launched July 2015 by Accela, Socrata, Zillow, BuildFax et al., with roughly a dozen cities/counties committed at launch. [A, historical] Current state, directly observed 2026-08-07: **permitdata.org returns 301 to mobojo.com** (an unrelated site — the domain lapsed) [O], and the GitHub repo README states "this data standard is not being actively maintained, and is provided for informational purposes" [O] (github.com/open-data-standards/permitdata.org). No maintained adopter list exists. BLDS is the canonical failed standard for this domain.
- **Open311** (the comparison case): still alive as a standard and monitored ecosystem — status.open311.org actively monitors dozens of GeoReport v2 endpoints with activity through late 2025, sustained by vendors (SeeClickFix/CivicPlus, Connected Bits) whose products implement it. Adoption measured in dozens of US cities, not thousands — partial success, vendor-carried. [M, informal] The lesson versus BLDS: Open311 had vendors whose commercial product WAS the standard interface; BLDS asked vendors to standardize data exhaust they had no revenue reason to publish.
- **Zoning/parcel schema standards**: no standard found with real US local-government adoption. OGC LandInfra (2016-17) surfaced in no US jurisdiction deployment found [A, absence]. The FGDC Cadastral Data Content Standard exists federally but does not bind local assessors [G, context]. The de facto standards are private: Regrid's Parcel Schema (100+ standardized columns) and Zoneomics' API schema — proprietary normalization layers doing the job the public standard never did. [V]

## 6. MCP-era (2025-2026)

**Finding: federal agencies and commercial aggregators are shipping MCP; no US city or county is. That absence is the finding.**

- **Federal**: US Digital Corps / GSA fellows piloted MCP servers for USASpending and CDC PLACES; LLM accuracy on queries went from 0% / 2.1% (no MCP) to ~95% (with MCP). The Census Bureau and Government Publishing Office have public MCP servers on GitHub; CMS and Treasury reference MCP deployments in 2025 AI use-case inventories. [G/M] (digitalcorps.gsa.gov/pdfs/MCP_Report.pdf; FedScoop 2026-02-04)
- **Commercial property data**: ATTOM launched an MCP server on 2026-01-29 — billed as the first large-scale real-estate-data MCP server, covering 158M properties (property detail, AVM, sales, tax, deed/mortgage, foreclosure, hazard, boundaries). Licensed/paid, not public. [V] (attomdata.com news 2026-01; WAV Group 2026-01-27) Smaller third-party property/zoning MCP servers exist (HTAG Property Intelligence; hobbyist repos). [V/A]
- **Jurisdictions and permitting vendors**: **no US city, county, or permitting vendor (Accela, Tyler, OpenGov, MyGov, CitizenServe) was found shipping an MCP server or any agent-declared interface for permit/zoning/property data as of 2026-08-07.** Honest absence after targeted search, not proof of nonexistence — but if a major vendor had shipped one, it would be marketed loudly (as ATTOM's was). [A, absence]

## The agent-consumption gap

Assemble the numbers and the shape is unambiguous. There are roughly 39,000 general-purpose local governments and 33,295 zoning jurisdictions. Machine access to their data consists of: ~5,000 government ArcGIS servers at all levels combined (most of them state/federal or geometry-only, weekly-retested because they break), on the order of 150-200 verified county parcel API endpoints anyone has bothered to catalog, a few dozen big-city Socrata portals, permitting-vendor APIs that are gated per-agency behind OAuth and professional-services contracts (with FOIA requests filed just to see the documentation), zero jurisdiction-served zoning-rules APIs, and one dead open standard whose domain now redirects to a squatter. Every functioning at-scale interface to this data — Regrid's 3,229 counties, Shovels' 2,750 jurisdictions, Zoneomics' claimed 20,000+ municipalities, ATTOM's 158M properties, the NZA's 9,000 hand-read jurisdictions — is a private reconstruction built by scraping, bulk-file wrangling, county-by-county outreach, or human beings reading a million pages of PDF. The jurisdictions themselves serve viewers; the machines are served by middlemen.

The MCP evidence sharpens this into a timing argument. The federal government's own pilot measured what happens when an LLM meets public data without a machine interface: 0-2% accuracy, jumping to 95% with an MCP server in front of the same data. That is a quantified statement that the entire local-government data layer — where the accuracy stakes are setbacks, permitted uses, and legal entitlements rather than trivia — currently sits at the 0-2% end for agent consumption. And the first movers filling that gap (ATTOM in January 2026, plus a scatter of aggregator-backed MCP servers) are all commercial licensees of privately reconstructed data. No jurisdiction and no permitting vendor has shipped an agent interface. The pattern from BLDS versus Open311 predicts why: standards and interfaces survive here only when a vendor's revenue depends on them, and no incumbent permitting vendor earns revenue from public machine access.

The practical implication for anyone building in this space: the gap between "data exists somewhere in a viewer" and "an agent can query it with provenance" is not closing from the government side on any observable timeline — the counting institutions are dead, the endpoints churn weekly, and half the permitting jurisdictions in the country are not even online. Whoever does the reconstruction work — acquisition, normalization, verification, and a stable agent-facing contract with citations — is not competing with jurisdictions' own APIs, because on the evidence of 2021-2026, essentially none exist and none are coming. The competition is other private reconstructions, and the differentiator is exactly the thing scrapers cannot fake: verified, current, per-jurisdiction ground truth with a maintenance loop, exposed through the interface agents actually consume.
