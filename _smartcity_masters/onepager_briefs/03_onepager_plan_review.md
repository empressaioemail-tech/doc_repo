# Plan Review — one-pager brief

The category with the clearest buyer money story. The draft led with the mechanism ("Submittals, Pre-Reviewed and Cited") and buried the buyer's actual reason. This page leads with money and capacity, then explains the mechanism.

## Audience and job

City manager. The reviewer is the daily user and must never feel replaced by anything on this page, but the purchase decision and the pain sit with the manager. Job: recognize the outsourcing spend and the backlog as their own problem, understand that submittals arriving pre-checked moves both, and trust that the reviewer stays in charge.

## Page anatomy and copy

### Hero

**Headline:** Send Less of Your Review Out the Door

**Subhead:** Submittals arrive pre-reviewed against your city's own adopted code, cited section by section. Your staff spend their time on judgment instead of first-pass error-catching, so more review stays in-house and the queue moves.

### Pain strip

Cities pay outside firms to do their plan review, not because they want to, but because plans examiners cannot be hired or kept. And review is the bottleneck everyone feels: cycle times stretch, applicants resubmit, staff answer the same code question over and over, and the council hears about it.

State the problem and its scale in the buyer's world. Never attach a dollar figure, a percentage, or a cycle-time number to what we deliver.

### Optional evidence strip (operator-gated; added 2026-08-07)

The Plan Review claims register permits third-party problem-scale context, and the filed research (`../whitespace_package/05_R15_plan_review_cycle_times.md`) now supplies sourced versions. If the operator approves an evidence strip under the pain strip, use only these, with the attribution printed:

1. Public contracts show cities paying outside review firms 75 to 80 percent of their own plan-review and permit fees, with fully outsourced building departments running $250,000 to $830,000 a year in named municipalities. (Public municipal contracts and council records, 2015-2024.)
2. One in-depth city audit found the median application spent 50 days in review, with correction rounds running roughly double the city's own targets, and only 29 percent of correction comments judged necessary. (Seattle Office of the City Auditor, 2023.)
3. The examiner workforce is aging out: an industry survey projected 80 percent of code officials retiring within 15 years, and federal projections show the occupation shrinking through 2034. (ICC/NIBS survey, 2014; US Bureau of Labor Statistics.)

These are statements about the market, never about our results, and each carries its attribution on the page. If the operator does not approve the strip, the prose pain strip stands alone. Default without a ruling: prose only.

### What it does: four cards

**Card 1: Do more with the staff you have.** Submittals arrive pre-reviewed, so your examiners spend their time on judgment rather than first-pass error-catching, and less of your review has to go to an outside firm.

**Card 2: Cited to your code, every time.** Findings point at the actual section of the code your city adopted, not a generic rule. Open any finding and see what it rests on.

**Card 3: Your reviewer's judgment governs.** The system proposes, cites, and remembers; your reviewer accepts, edits, or overrides, and their decision is recorded with their reasoning. And when the system cannot judge something, it says so rather than guessing.

**Card 4: Your decisions stay yours.** How your city has decided things becomes part of your record, so consistency does not depend on who is at the desk that day, and the reasoning is still there years later.

### One-record band

**Header:** Every decision lands on the record of the place.

**Body:** A review decision and the property it concerns live in the same record your dashboards read, so the development services view shows what is in flight, what is stuck, and what was decided, all from one source. The comment letter comes out of the same flow.

### Smart reports band (optional)

Plan review sits alongside the smart reports the platform produces: studies a city would otherwise commission or go without. Two are live today: the Smart Site X-ray, the full read on a place, and the flood and drainage study, which answers what happens to a property in a heavy rain.

Never enumerate beyond these two. Never use Smart Site professional framing (realtors, developers) on this page.

The CTA wording matters: the city's own adopted local code is the safe demonstration material. No demonstration or screenshot may show licensed model-code body text before the publisher agreement is signed.
