1. What we are building, in one paragraph

For twenty years software was sold to people: a login, a monthly seat, a screen to click through. That model is breaking, because the thing increasingly using software is not a person but an automated assistant, an agent, that reads instructions and does the work itself. We are building the supply layer those agents draw on for one specific subject: everything that legally and physically bears on a piece of land. Zoning, building codes, flood exposure, soils, ownership, liens, easements, permit history, and so on. When an agent or one of our own apps needs to know what governs a property, it asks our system and gets back not a stack of documents but a reasoned, cited answer that carries its own evidence. Two companies sit over this. Hauska Inc. owns the underlying engine, data contract, and settlement layer (the substrate). Empressa is the product brand whose apps ride on top. The buyer of the substrate is the agent operator; the buyer of the apps is the professional (a broker, an architect, a city).

2. The technology stack, in plain terms

Software is built in layers. Here are ours, described the way you would describe the parts of an organization rather than the wiring. Most of the individual building blocks are standard and off the shelf. The novelty is in how they are combined and what the combination does, which is the distinction that matters for patent strategy.

The apps (what users touch). A family of product surfaces, each built for a different professional but all drawing on the same engine: the Property Brief (a broker points at an address and gets a structured report), Codex (building-code lookup and plan review), AEC-cortex (design and compliance tools for architects), SmartCity OS (city government operations), and Radar (investor deal screening). Built with standard web and mobile tooling.

The gate (the controlled doorway). Everything that leaves the system passes through a single checkpoint. It verifies credentials, decides which product and which customer is asking and at what tier, meters the request so it can be accounted for and billed, and enforces what each caller is allowed to see. For someone with a classification and need-to-know background, this is the access-control layer turned into infrastructure. It speaks an open agent protocol (MCP), so an automated agent can connect to it directly the way one program talks to another.

The engine (turns records into reasoning). The machinery that reads raw records and reasons over them. It has two halves: a property and parcel engine (flood, soil, hydrology, zoning, ownership, hazard) and a plan-review engine (building-code retrieval and compliance findings). They compose: a plan review draws on the property context beneath it.

The place graph (how knowledge is organized). Rather than a flat database, knowledge is arranged as a graph: a location at the center, with everything that bears on it hanging off as sourced connections. Detailed in Section 5.

The atom contract (the shape everything speaks). A single self-describing unit of knowledge, the atom, is the common language every layer uses. It is the backbone of the product's trustworthiness and our core proprietary method. Detailed in Section 5.

The settlement layer (the SDK). A payment and proof layer that meters usage, routes payment, and anchors a tamper-evident record of what was consumed onto a blockchain. This is how value flows back to the people whose data drove an answer. Detailed in Section 6.

3. How people actually use it

There are two front doors. An automated agent connects through the open protocol, asks its question, and receives a cited answer it can act on. A human uses one of the apps and gets the same underlying intelligence rendered as a readable report. In both cases the answer has the same shape: a conclusion, the reasoning behind it, the sources cited, a confidence figure, and a timestamp.

5. The knowledge-atom system - our core proprietary method

This is the most important section for patent purposes. It describes the method we believe is genuinely novel and that the whole product's trustworthiness rests on. It is domain-agnostic: we apply it to land and jurisdiction, but nothing about it is specific to that domain.

5.1 The problem it solves

Ordinary AI assistants hand you a bare answer ("this parcel is in flood zone AE") with no way to know where it came from, how sure the system really is, whether that confidence was earned or just guessed, or how to check it. For something that informs a real-estate or legal decision, that is not good enough. It cannot be trusted, audited, priced, or improved over time.

5.2 The atom - knowledge that describes itself

Our solution is a rule enforced everywhere in the system: no piece of knowledge ever travels as a bare fact. Every unit of knowledge, an atom, carries six things bundled with it, and nothing is released without them.

Two design choices here are unusual and defensible. First, the confidence is never a naked number. It carries how many observations it rests on, how wide the uncertainty is, and how it was earned. A bare "92%" is exactly what we refuse to ship. Second, the access policy travels inside the atom itself (public-free, public-paid, internal-only, private-to-one-customer, or shared-between-customers). Confidentiality and commercial terms are properties of the fact, not a setting on a server. The marking rides with the document. And atoms come in families: a data atom is a fact about the world, but other families record a decision a human made, an action a system took, or a recorded legal instrument such as an easement or lien. The system records its own reasoning and history in the same format it records facts.

5.3 The place graph - how atoms connect

The atoms are not a flat pile. They hang off a location. The result is not a map with pins; it is a location surrounded by everything that legally and physically bears on it, where every connecting line is itself a sourced relationship with its own chain of custody. A question becomes a guided walk across these connections, and we charge by the reasoning walk, not by the document returned.

5.4 Confidence is earned, not asserted - the calibration loop

The single most distinctive idea: the system does not make up its confidence numbers. It earns them over time by tracking whether its past claims actually came true. A claim starts at an honest, clearly labeled baseline and is then sharpened by real outcomes through a four-step loop. Over time the confidence numbers stop being guesses and become track records.

5.5 Two safeguards built in from the start

  • Isolation. One customer's private results never pool into anyone else's confidence numbers. Each customer has a partitioned private ledger; only anonymous, explicitly shared signal feeds the shared model. This is both a trust commitment and, we believe, part of what is defensible.
  • Honesty fallback. Until a claim-type has enough real outcomes to be trustworthy, the atom openly shows a baseline confidence labeled "asserted," never a confident-looking number dressed up as if it had been earned.

5.6 Two refinements that strengthen the method

Point-in-time honesty (no hindsight). Each fact is stamped not only with when it is true in the world but with when we recorded it, and the system is built so any historical analysis or earned-confidence calculation can use only what was known at that earlier time. This prevents the system from grading itself with hindsight, the single most common way performance claims are inflated. A track record computed this way is honest by construction.

Stable identity, not labels. The thing a fact attaches to (a property here, and in the financial embodiment a security or a wallet) is identified by a permanent internal identifier, never by a label that can change or be reused. A resolver maintains that identity across renamings, splits, and merges. Without it, history silently corrupts the first time a label changes hands; with it, the compounding record stays trustworthy over years.

Why this matters for IP: the atom format, the earned-confidence calibration loop, and the two safeguards are domain-agnostic methods. We believe this cluster is the one most worth examining for utility-patent protection. In honesty for your framing: the loop is built and live in its capture form, but confidence on the live read path is still asserted at a baseline today, not yet fully earned, which is the difference between a method reduced to practice and one still being reduced to practice.

6. The temporal-context engine - explaining the past and anticipating the future

This is the second core proprietary method, built on the first. The knowledge-atom system compounds what is and was true. The temporal-context engine adds the other two tenses: a grounded explanation of why something happened, and a grounded, calibrated anticipation of what is pending that could change the future. We believe the combination is novel and a strong utility-patent candidate.

6.1 The problem it solves

An ordinary AI asked "what is coming that could affect this property" searches the open world and returns ungrounded guesses with no source and no track record. For a decision that turns on it, that is unusable. The engine replaces open-world guessing with a bounded, cited, calibratable method.

6.2 The key design choice: a curated source registry, not open-world search

Instead of letting the system roam the internet, we define, per domain, the authoritative forward-looking sources and monitor only those. This changes the machine's job from "predict the future" to "read these specific authoritative sources and extract what is pending," which is grounded and citable. In the property domain those sources are things like state and local legislature dockets, planning-commission and zoning-board agendas, federal rulemaking, and housing-authority notices.

Concrete example: a bill pending in a state legislature that would retroactively reclassify certain multifamily properties as assisted housing. An investor needs that, with a probability and an effective date, before it is reflected in the market. The engine captures the pending item the moment it appears, links it to the specific properties it would affect, and tracks it to resolution.

Explaining the past and anticipating the future are the same system in opposite tenses, sharing three parts: capture (persist authoritative events as they appear), materiality (compute whether an event actually moved the subject, honestly, accounting for other things happening at the same time), and calibration (compare predictions to outcomes so confidence is earned). A pending item captured today becomes the historical explanation tomorrow once it resolves. One consequence worth stating plainly: nothing can be explained historically that was not captured, so capture has to start early; uncaptured time is permanently unexplainable.

6.4 Confidence is two separate track records

The engine keeps two independent earned track records, because two different things can be right or wrong: whether a pending item resolves as predicted (the bill passes on schedule), and whether, once it does, it actually affects the subject as expected. Keeping them separate stops a mistake in one from hiding inside the other.

Because the engine makes predictions that later resolve, it accumulates a track record, and we make that track record provable rather than merely claimed. Periodically the system seals a cryptographic fingerprint of its records to a public, independent timestamping layer (anchored ultimately to a public blockchain), with no token and no payments involved. Anyone can later verify that a given prediction existed, unaltered, before its outcome was known. This converts "trust our track record" into "verify it independently," and is itself a candidate method.

Why this matters for IP, and the cross-domain point. The curated-source-registry forward layer, the shared capture, materiality, and calibration engine, the two-track calibration, and the verifiable point-in-time track record are domain-agnostic methods. They are being reduced to practice in this property and jurisdictional system and, in parallel, in a financial-markets application. Two concrete embodiments of one method in unrelated fields is strong evidence the invention is a general technical mechanism rather than an abstract idea tied to one business, which is a meaningful strength for the filing. The method should be owned at the substrate level (Hauska), with each application a licensed embodiment.

7. How we help data owners protect and monetize their data, and where the blockchain comes in

A core part of the thesis is that we do not treat data owners (cities, code bodies like ICC, commercial-data firms, and individual contributors) as commodity inputs to be acquired cheaply and resold. We treat them as licensors who keep control of their data and get paid when it drives an answer. Four mechanisms make that real, and the last is where the blockchain settlement layer enters.

  • Control rides inside the data. Because every atom carries its own access policy and commercial terms (Section 5.2), a source defines who may see its data and on what terms, and that marking is enforced at the gate on every single request. The owner does not have to trust a server configuration.
  • Sovereignty. A source's or customer's private data never pools into a shared or public asset. Private stays private; only what is explicitly shared contributes to anything common.
  • Pay-per-use revenue share. When a source's data drives a metered answer, the system is designed to route that source its share automatically, rather than relying on an annual lump-sum license. Small, frequent, usage-based settlement replaces the old all-you-can-eat contract.
  • Proof and settlement on a blockchain (the SDK). The settlement layer wraps a licensed dataset as a verifiable data asset, anchors a tamper-evident record of each consumption event onto a public blockchain, and settles payment in a stablecoin (USDC, on Base, Ethereum, and Polygon). The owner gets an independent, auditable record of exactly how their data was used and gets paid against it. The blockchain is doing two jobs: an immutable usage receipt (protect) and a fast, low-cost settlement rail for many tiny payments (monetize).

So the patentable subject matter here spans methods reduced to practice (verifiable usage anchoring, stablecoin settlement) and methods still in development (automated per-source revenue routing).

8. Other distinctive features worth your attention

Flagged for your professional judgment on what is protectable and how, not asserting legal novelty ourselves.

  1. Sell the reasoning, not the data. The raw public records stay free; the paid product is the interpreted, cited, reasoned answer over them, and the architecture is built so no output ever leaves as a bare fact.
  2. Agent-first distribution. The system is built for automated agents to consume directly through an open protocol, with the human apps as a second surface, the reverse of the usual build-a-website-then-add-an-API order.
  3. The place graph as a billable surface. Modeling jurisdictional reality as a location with sourced connections, and charging per reasoning walk rather than per document, is a novel commercial and technical framing.
  4. Access and commercial terms embedded in the unit of data (the atom's access policy), enforced at a single gate on every request.
  5. Verifiable, blockchain-anchored usage with automated per-source settlement (Section 7), a method for paying data contributors per use with an independent audit trail.
  6. Stable-identity resolution across renamings, splits, and merges (Section 5.6), an entity-resolution method that keeps a compounding record from corrupting when an identifier changes or is reused.
  7. Point-in-time correctness that prevents hindsight (Sections 5.6 and 6), so an earned track record cannot be inflated by grading against information that did not exist yet.
  8. The temporal-context engine (Section 6): a curated-source-registry forward layer plus a shared capture, materiality, and calibration engine that explains the past and anticipates the future on the same machinery, with two independent track records.
  9. Lineage-aware data rights. A derived result is serveable only if every source in its input lineage permits derived use; the right travels with the data through every computation and is enforced at the gate.
  10. One method, two embodiments. The atom, calibration, temporal-context, and verifiable-track-record methods are being reduced to practice in this jurisdictional system and, in parallel, in a financial-markets application, evidence the invention is general rather than tied to one field.
