Introduction

Hauska: making data AI-native

Most data is built for people and apps: rows, documents, API calls. An AI agent can read it but cannot trust it, because it arrives bare, with no source, no measure of certainty, no terms of use. Hauska fixes that one level down, at the fact itself.

It turns every fact into a self-describing, AI-native unit that carries where it came from, how sure it is and how that was earned, a citation, when it is true, and who may use it. Those units connect into a graph, reasoning runs over them, and a settlement layer pays the sources whose data drove an answer. The result is data an agent can consume, trust, verify, and pay for on its own.

What you are about to read, in order: the four-layer architecture, the loop that makes its confidence earned and provable, the same machine running across three industries, and a real-estate deep dive that follows one parcel through every layer.

The loop, part 1 of 2

Confidence is earned, and the record is provable

Two mechanisms make a number trustworthy and they run on the same claim. The cycle on the left earns the confidence by comparing past calls to real outcomes. The chain on the right proves it, by sealing the call when it is made. Two guardrails keep both honest.

The confidence is never a bare number: it carries how many observations it rests on (n), how wide the uncertainty is, and how it was earned (basis). Sealing at emission, plus anchoring the whole population of calls (not just the winners), is what makes the track record impossible to backdate or cherry-pick.

The loop, part 2 of 2

Bounded to a curated registry, never open-web guessing; data that matches nothing we track is noted, not forced onto the graph. Two ledgers because two different things can be wrong: whether the event happens, and whether, once it does, it actually moves the subject. The materiality step measures the marginal move, net of everything else happening at the same time.

Why it compounds, part 1 of 2

The nodes, and the methods, compound together

Every node connects to shared ones (codes, jurisdictions, issuers), one event fans out across domains, and every outcome feeds one shared calibration. Two named effects live in this picture: the data and outcomes compound, and the engines themselves get sharper across every domain at once.

Contribution flywheel: usage is contribution; every outcome from anywhere sharpens the confidence of that claim type for everyone, so more use makes it better, not just more nodes. Cross-domain method transfer: the calibration math, the event-impact model, and the resolver improve across domains at once, the model hardened on markets lifts real estate, which almost no data company gets because their methods are domain-locked.

Why it compounds, part 2 of 2

A marketplace for verifiable digital assets

The same metering and settlement rail opens into a two-sided market. Many sellers list verifiable digital assets; many buyers pay per use; the marketplace verifies, meters, matches, settles, and anchors. More buyers attract more sellers and the reverse, the two-sided flywheel.

Two-sided flywheel: more consumers create more usage and revenue, which attracts more data providers because they get paid, which enriches the catalog, which attracts more consumers. Plus a liquidity effect: more buyers and sellers mean better price discovery, which pulls in more of both. Each asset class (data, signals, asset rights) runs its own version of this market.
