# Flood study, hotel occupancy tax, and plan review

What each one does for the people using it.

---

## Flood and drainage study

**The question it answers is the one you asked: when it rains four inches, what happens to this
property?**

Today that question takes a phone call, a wait, and often a paid engineering report. The study
answers it on screen in the time it takes to make coffee.

**Run the scenarios side by side.** One inch, two, four. Change the rainfall depth and the answer
redraws — where the water collects, how much of the lot it covers, which way it leaves, and where
it goes next. Comparing two storms is one click, not two studies.

**Screen everything at once, not one property at a time.** Instead of looking a single address
up, it can take every permit currently in review and rank them by which sit on ground that ponds.
A reviewer sees the three that need a closer look before opening any of them.

**It keeps the regulatory answer and the modelled answer apart.** The FEMA flood zone is a
regulatory line and the drainage study is what the ground actually does with water. They answer
different questions, and the screen never blends them into one number. A property outside the
FEMA zone that still ponds is exactly the case worth catching, and it is shown as two facts
rather than a contradiction.

**It does not replace your engineer. It tells you whether you need one.** That is the real value
and it is a budget question, not a technical one. Before committing to a report, you can see
whether there is anything to investigate. When there is, you commission the report knowing why.
When there is not, you have your answer.

**Who uses it.** Development Services during permit review. Engineering as a first pass before
scoping work. Fire and EMS to understand where water goes during an event. The City Manager when
a decision needs a defensible basis.

## Hotel occupancy tax

**A read-only window onto what has been filed, what has been claimed, and how it reconciles against
what the city actually booked.**

**What has come in.** Each filing, what was reported, what was paid, and how the current period
compares with the last twelve months. The trend is on the page rather than assembled by hand each
quarter.

**What was claimed.** Exemptions are shown alongside the filings that claim them, so a pattern is
visible rather than buried in individual returns.

**Who has not filed, subject to one open question.** A list of filings tells you who did file.
Knowing who is missing is the harder question and the more valuable one. Whether this feed can
answer it depends on whether the source provides a way to identify a taxpayer across filings —
an open question with the vendor, and scoped separately for that reason.

**Where the numbers came from.** Every figure names the system it came from and the date it was
read, so a question in a council meeting has an answer in the room.

**Who uses it.** Finance, primarily — this is their day-to-day view. The City Manager for the
revenue picture without asking Finance to pull it together.

**One note on scope.** This reads what the source system provides. It is a reporting and
reconciliation view, not a replacement for the filing system itself.

## Plan review

**It sits beside MyGov rather than replacing it.** Your staff keep reviewing the way they review
today. This runs alongside as a fast first pass, so nothing changes overnight and nobody has to
relearn their job to benefit from it.

**It shortens the cycle.** A review that takes two weeks today gets a first pass back in hours.
The reviewer opens a submittal already knowing which items need their attention, instead of
starting from a blank sheet every time.

**Every department reviews its own scope at the same time.** Fire looks at fire. Zoning looks at
zoning. Structural, Public Works and Engineering the same. They work in parallel rather than
queuing behind one another, which is where most of the calendar time actually goes.

**Every finding names the code it came from.** Not "this looks wrong" but the actual section, and
the applicant's correction letter carries the same citation. Fewer arguments, and a defensible
record.

**It tells you what it could not check.** A rule it has no way to evaluate is marked as
unevaluated rather than quietly passed. A reviewer knows exactly where their own judgement is
still required, which is what makes the fast pass safe to rely on.

**The applicant gets one letter.** Findings from every department are consolidated into a single
correction notice, instead of three letters arriving on different days from different people. And
when the applicant resubmits, the next cycle shows what changed and what did not.

**Who uses it.** Development Services runs the cycle. Fire and EMS, Public Works, Engineering and
Planning each work their own findings. The applicant sees one consolidated response.

## Where they meet

These three are not separate products. They sit in the same platform, behind the same login, with
access limited by department.

A permit in review can carry a drainage result, a plan review finding and its supporting documents
on the same record. Fire sees its review and not Finance's filings. Finance sees the tax picture
and not the plan sets. The City Manager sees across all of it.

That is the point of doing it in one system rather than three.
